# Story craft: every type we practise

This is the reference for the story track of the learning engine. Each type has the same five parts: what it is, its shape, an original example sized for our formats (a 10–30 s Reel or a Think! scene), the usual mistake, and the checks the critic uses (also listed in `curriculum.json`). The examples are ours. The ideas are standard craft, found in screenwriting, comedy-writing and narrative-design practice, not invented here.

**One rule above all:** write for the eye first. Our films are animation, often watched with the sound off, so every beat has to read as an action on screen.

---

## Foundations

### Logline and premise
- **What it is:** the whole story in one sentence. *Who* wants *what*, *what stands in the way*, and *why it's funny or matters*.
- **The engine:** a good premise has an engine. It is a situation that can keep producing scenes, not just one joke.
- **Shape:** [character] + [concrete want] + [obstacle] + [the twist of tone].
- **Example:** *A cook in a kitchen beside a concert hall wants a quiet service, but every sound check shakes the kitchen apart.* The engine is that each sound check is a new disaster.
- **Mistake:** a theme or topic instead of a story ("a video about noise").
- **Check:** someone else can retell it after one read.

### Want, need and flaw
- **What they are:**
  - *Want:* the visible goal (finish the soufflé).
  - *Need:* what would actually help (let go of control).
  - *Flaw:* the habit that causes the trouble (fixing things that aren't broken).
- **Rules:**
  - Show the want through action: he guards the oven with a ladle.
  - Comedy often comes from the flaw making the want impossible.
- **Mistake:** a character who announces the want in dialogue.

### Setup and payoff
- **What it is:** plant something early, use it later. Viewers love it because rewatching shows it was there all along.
- **Rules:**
  - Disguise the plant as something else. The spoon is first just a spoon in a joke about caramel.
  - Every plant must pay off, and every payoff must be planted.
- **Example:** A03 plants the spoon; A11 delivers a single spoon in a bag.
- **Mistake:** a payoff that relies on something never shown (a cheat), or a plant so loud it gives the joke away.

## Comedy structures

### Rule of three (pattern, pattern, break)
- **What it is:** two beats set a pattern, and the third breaks it.
- **Rules:** the break must be fair (it follows the rules of the world) and short. It is the fastest beat.
- **Example:** thump 1 rattles the pans, thump 2 rattles the pans, thump 3 rattles only Dev.
- **Mistake:** a third beat that is just bigger, not different. That's escalation, which is a separate tool.

### Escalation
- **What it is:** each beat raises the stakes or the absurdity over the one before.
- **Rules:** plan the ladder backwards from the top, and the top lands just before the end.
- **Example:** a drip, then a puddle, then a wave carries Dev out the door.
- **Mistake:** a flat ladder, with two beats at the same level.

### Joke craft: misdirection and the turn
- **What it is:** the setup leads the audience to one meaning, then the punch reveals another.
- **Rules:**
  - Put the punch word or image last.
  - In animation the turn is often a cut, a reveal by camera, or a reaction shot.
- **Example:** "I'll handle the bass" (we expect the music); cut to Dev holding a fish.
- **Mistake:** explaining the joke after it lands.

### Running gag and callback
- **What it is:** a gag that returns, changed each time. The final callback tops the others.
- **Rule:** a newcomer must still get the last one, so it carries its own setup in miniature.
- **Example:** the dialogue drill. Moss says "Mm." in every line; at the end the waiter copies Moss's one tiny shoulder bob.
- **Mistake:** repeating it unchanged (diminishing returns), or a callback that needs the earlier episode.

### Irony, deadpan and contradiction
- **What it is:** words and actions contradict on screen. The deadpan character never signals the joke.
- **Rule:** someone else voices the reaction (a chorus character, a reaction shot), the way a laugh track would.
- **Example:** "Mm. I am dancing." Nothing moves, except one tiny shoulder bob.
- **Mistake:** the deadpan character smirks, which explains the joke.

### Endings: button, topper and loop
- **Topper:** the last beat is the biggest.
- **Button:** a quiet beat just after the payoff (a look to camera, one sigh).
- **Loop:** the last frame flows into the first, which suits Reels.
- **Rule:** nothing happens after the payoff except the button.
- **Mistake:** a trailing beat that deflates the laugh.

## Story shapes

### Three-act structure (micro)
- **Shape:**
  - Act 1: setup and the inciting change.
  - Act 2: confrontation, where attempts fail and pressure rises.
  - Act 3: climax and resolution.
- **Short-form timing:** in a 20 s Reel that is about 4 s, 12 s and 4 s.
- **Rules:**
  - The turn into act 2 is a choice by the lead.
  - The resolution answers the question the opening asked.

### Kishōtenketsu (intro, development, turn, reconcile)
- **What it is:** a four-beat shape common in East Asian storytelling. It needs no conflict and no villain. Beat 3 introduces something that seems unrelated, and beat 4 shows how it connects.
- **Why it suits us:** it is ideal for calm, observational Reels.
- **Example:**
  1. Dev stirs a pot.
  2. He tastes and adds salt, carefully.
  3. Across the hall, the bassist tunes one low note, again and again.
  4. Dev's stirring has fallen into the bassist's rhythm; he notices, smiles, and keeps time.
- **Mistake:** forcing a conflict into beat 3, which turns it back into a three-act story.

### Hero's journey (compressed)
- **Shape:** a call, doubt or refusal, crossing a threshold, an ordeal, then a return with something gained.
- **Short form:** compress it to a beat each. Dev is asked to cook for the band, hesitates, steps into the green room, survives the drummer's order, and returns with a signed spatula.
- **Mistake:** rushing the ordeal. It is the beat that earns the return.

### Hook and cold open
- **What it is:** something changes or is wrong in the first second, and it raises a question only the ending answers.
- **Rule:** no logo, title or slow setup before it.
- **Example:** frame 1 shows a soufflé floating. Why? The answer is the bass drop.
- **Mistake:** a hook that is answered right away. The tension is gone.

## Genre tools

### Curiosity gap and mystery
- **What it is:** plant an open question early and give fair, visible clues. The answer should feel surprising yet inevitable.
- **Example:** spoons keep vanishing from the kitchen; the clues are tiny clinks from the drum kit.
- **Mistake:** clues the viewer couldn't have seen, or an answer that contradicts them.

### Suspense (the audience knows more)
- **What it is:** suspense, in Hitchcock's bomb-under-the-table sense. The audience sees the danger the character doesn't, and time pressure is visible.
- **Rule:** the release comes from the character's own action.
- **Example:** we see the amp's volume knob creeping up while Dev balances a tower of plates.
- **Mistake:** surprise instead of suspense, where the danger is hidden until it hits.

### Horror-lite tension and release
- **What it is:** a normal space becomes wrong in small steps. Silence and stillness set up the scare.
- **Our tone:** follow the peak with a comic release.
- **Example:** the kitchen lights hum, the pans stop rattling, and a slow creak comes from the walk-in fridge. It's the drummer, asking for ice.

### Heartfelt emotional arc
- **What it is:** the lead ends in a different emotional state, shown in one concrete action rather than told by music or narration.
- **Example:** Dev, irritated by the noise all episode, quietly saves a plate for the exhausted roadie.
- **Mistake:** sentiment that isn't earned. The change needs a cause we saw.

### Dialogue with subtext
- **What it is:** characters never state their feelings directly; they say something else, and the action carries the meaning.
- **Rules:**
  - The scene should work with the sound off.
  - Each line must be something a real person would say.
- **Example:** "The soup's fine." (He's hurt; he scrapes the pot harder.)

## Formats we make

### Explainer story (Think!)
- **What it is:** the viewer-first rule applied as story.
- **Shape:**
  1. Open on the viewer's problem.
  2. Explain one mechanism through a concrete scene with a character.
  3. Leave the viewer knowing what to do.
- **Where it works:** the curiosity gap and setup and payoff work inside explainers too.
- **Rules:** the fact lock and the evidence rules always apply.

### Series format (story on rails)
- **What it is:** one beat grammar reused across many premises. Viewers learn the format once and then enjoy it every time.
- **Example:** reference-ig-01 and ig-04 run the same 26 s grammar: a hit, a surface sign, a roll inside, rewind, fast clock, back out, healing.
- **Rules:** name what changes per episode and what never changes. The format must be recognisable within 2 seconds.

## How a story drill runs
1. `learn.py next` picks the story skill (`logline` comes first; the others unlock from it).
2. Start the drill with this guide, or with a reference video studied for structure, as the reference:
   ```bash
   learn.py start <skill> .claude/skills/animation-learning-engine/references/story-craft.md
   ```
3. Write the piece to `draft.md`. It needs three parts: the logline, a beat sheet with seconds per beat, and the on-screen action for each beat.
4. Log the draft:
   ```bash
   learn.py measure <drill> draft.md
   ```
5. The critic reads it as an outsider and gives a verdict for each check, quoting the beat as evidence:
   ```bash
   learn.py critique <drill> verdicts.json
   ```
6. Revise up to 3 times, then `close`, recording what was learned.
7. A story skill reaches level 4 only when a produced episode uses it and the owner has reviewed it.
