# Creative development: earn attention, then repay it

Use this after extracting source-grounded candidates and before finalizing briefs. Expand the ways to explain an idea, not the facts themselves. An intriguing framing cannot rescue an unsupported claim. The research rubric decides evidence readiness; this pass decides how to make the supported lesson worth watching.

## 1. Find the viewer's unresolved question

For each finalist, write one specific viewer situation, the question it creates, the mistaken or incomplete mental model, and the useful change in understanding. Describe a person doing something, not a demographic label: “someone who rereads a chapter but cannot explain it” is more useful than “people interested in self-improvement.”

Identify the emotional stake without inventing danger: wasted effort, a confusing tradeoff, an unexplained everyday experience, a consequential decision, or the pleasure of finally understanding a mechanism. The video must deliver a lesson, not merely intensify the emotion.

## 2. Diverge: generate twelve possible angles

Make twelve short angle cards across these lenses. Use a lens only when it fits; do not force twelve different factual claims from one remark.

| Lens | Creative question |
| --- | --- |
| Hidden mechanism | What invisible process explains the visible result? |
| Counterintuitive finding | Which reasonable expectation does the verified evidence challenge? |
| Everyday friction | Where does this idea show up in an ordinary day? |
| Visual experiment | What can the viewer try, predict, or compare? |
| Scale change | What becomes understandable when we zoom in or out? |
| Mental model | What simple representation makes the idea usable? |
| Boundary case | When does the familiar advice stop working? |
| Competing explanations | What evidence separates two plausible accounts? |
| Misconception | What precise belief needs correction, and who actually holds it? |
| Tradeoff | What do we gain and give up with each choice? |
| Discovery story | What observation changed someone's understanding, if documented? |
| Future possibility | What could follow if stated assumptions hold? Label speculation. |

Each card contains a working title, one-sentence promise, source anchor, proposed visual, and evidence gap. Imagined scenes and analogies are narrative devices. Label hypothetical situations; never invent experiments, quotations, people, historical events, results, or scientific mechanisms. State where an analogy breaks down.

## 3. Converge: develop three genuinely different concepts

Select three frames per finalist, such as **viewer challenge**, **mechanism reveal**, and **decision/tradeoff**. Different adjectives on the same title are not different concepts.

For each, supply the viewer question, claim boundary, opening visual, teaching arc, and reason to choose it over the other two. Compare accessible existing videos by what they explain, not by superficial editing style. Specify the added value: a clearer demonstration, missing qualification, better example, new evidence, or a useful synthesis. When coverage is inaccessible, call originality an untested hypothesis.

Choose one concept using five editorial tests: immediate comprehension, specific benefit, honest tension, visual teachability, and achievable payoff within the proposed runtime. Mark pass/weak/fail with reasons; these are editorial judgments, not predicted CTR. Reject any concept that depends on misleading omission. Preserve the runners-up as testable alternatives.

## 4. Write three complete opening hooks

For every selected topic, write three distinct spoken openings of approximately 45–70 words, with matching shot directions. Deliver finished language, not “insert shocking fact here.” Use these as planning windows rather than rigid retention rules:

| Time | Narration job | Visual job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 seconds | Present the concrete question, challenge, or surprising verified contrast. | Show the object, result, or decision immediately. |
| 5–15 seconds | Make the tension intelligible and personally relevant. | Let the viewer predict, compare, or notice something. |
| 15–30 seconds | State the precise learning payoff and begin delivering it. | Reveal the first useful relationship; transition into the explanation. |

No logo preamble, biography dump, unsupported superlative, fake urgency, or generic “Have you ever wondered?” Delay neither the subject nor its relevance. Curiosity comes from a meaningful missing explanation; do not withhold basic context just to manufacture mystery. A title's factual qualification must survive in the opening.

### Illustrative hooks: a video about explaining what you read

These are fictional packaging examples, not verified learning-science claims. Research is required before making claims about which study method works better.

**Challenge:** “Think of the last useful thing you read. Now explain it in one sentence, without looking back. Where did you get stuck? That gap is our starting point. In this video, we’ll separate recognizing an explanation from producing one, then build a small test you can use to see what you can actually explain.”

*Visual:* A paragraph folds away; a blank speech bubble appears. Show a viewer answer as an explicitly hypothetical example, not a measured result.

**Mechanism:** “Here is a page with all the answers. Beside it is the same page with only the questions. Reading one and answering the other are different tasks. But what does each task reveal? We’ll walk through a short example, inspect the gaps, and distinguish what this little exercise can tell us from what it cannot.”

*Visual:* Split-screen answer sheet/question sheet. Animate one concrete explanation being built, with no invented brain scan or scientific diagram.

**Decision:** “You have ten minutes before you close your notes. You could read another page, revisit the last one, or try explaining an idea without the text. Which task fits what you need next? We’ll compare the questions each task answers, then turn that comparison into a simple decision you can make for your own material.”

*Visual:* Three task cards beside a ten-minute planning clock. Do not display fabricated performance results.

## 5. Build title–thumbnail pairs

Deliver three pairs, one per concept, and recommend one. A title names the question or benefit; its thumbnail supplies a concrete visual tension rather than repeating the entire title. Use one focal idea, readable mobile composition, and little text. Describe the actual frame, labels, contrast, and visual question so Claude Code can build it.

Reveal enough to earn an informed click without spoiling the entire explanatory journey. Avoid vague mystery, dishonest before/after imagery, invented authority quotes, and claims stronger than the sources. Example: **“You Read It. Can You Explain It?”** paired with an answer-filled page becoming an empty speech bubble. “This Hack Makes You a Genius” promises an unsupported outcome.

## 6. Design retention around learning

Create a promise–payoff map: each opening promise, the scene that fulfills it, the evidence used, and how the viewer recognizes resolution. Pay off a small promise early; let that answer create the next genuine question. Close every important loop before the ending.

For each scene, state what changes in the viewer's understanding, why they should continue, and the likely confusion or exit point. Use a prediction, worked example, counterexample, scale change, or visual reveal when it advances the lesson. Avoid arbitrary “pattern interrupts every 30 seconds,” perpetual teasing, or unrelated spectacle. Let difficult explanations breathe.

Use one recurring visual object when it helps continuity. Distinguish illustrative motion from measured data. End with a useful application or sharper question, not a recap padded to reach a runtime.

## 7. Stress-test and hand off

Write the boring version of the premise, then improve its specificity. **Boring:** “The importance of learning.” **Concrete:** “Can you explain yesterday's chapter without opening it?” The improvement is a visible task and clear question, not louder adjectives.

Check the selected concept with muted first frames, audio-only narration, and small-screen thumbnail previews when tools permit. Otherwise label these as planned checks. Test understanding and expectation: can someone identify the subject and expected payoff? Do not invent viewer feedback.

Include a 30-second pilot storyboard and one alternative hook in the production handoff. If real channel analytics later become available, compare title/thumbnail tests and opening retention using observed data and stated context. Change one main variable per test where practical; small or uncontrolled results are directional, not causal proof. No universal benchmark or guaranteed improvement applies.
